# Vegetarian Indian Diets: Micronutrients That Need Attention

## Introduction

A vegetarian Indian diet can meet an athlete's needs, but a few nutrients need deliberate attention because of **how well the body absorbs them from plant foods**, not because plant foods lack them. Cereal-and-legume diets are rich in iron and zinc on paper while delivering less of both in practice.

**Boundaries.** This is general educational information about foods and absorption. It is not a diagnosis, not a claim about any individual, and not a supplement recommendation. Nothing here should be used to infer that a particular athlete is deficient in anything — that requires bloodwork and a qualified clinician. Supplementation decisions belong with a doctor or registered dietitian, never with a meal planner.

## Why Absorption, Not Just Content, Matters

Iron comes in two dietary forms: haem (animal foods) and non-haem (plant foods). **The iron in Indian diets is mainly non-haem**, and its absorption depends heavily on what else is in the same meal. FAO's review of Indian diets reports the bioavailability of dietary iron from plant foods as roughly **1–6 percent** (Narasinga Rao et al., 1983, cited in FAO 2003) — so the iron in a food table and the iron actually absorbed can differ greatly. The same broad pattern applies to zinc.

## What Reduces Absorption

- **Phytates** (grains, legumes, nuts, seeds; higher in unrefined or under-milled cereals than refined ones) **can decrease non-haem iron absorption by 51–82 percent** (FAO 2003).
- **Polyphenols and tannins** in tea, coffee, cocoa, some spices and vegetables bind iron. **Polyphenols in tea are strong inhibitors** (FAO 2003) — relevant given how common tea with meals is.
- **Calcium** (milk, cheese) and some soy proteins also inhibit non-haem iron absorption.

## What Improves Absorption

**Vitamin C is the most effective lever.** Ascorbic acid is the most potent enhancer of non-haem iron absorption *even in the presence of phytates, tannates and calcium*, reducing ferric iron to the better-absorbed ferrous form by 75–98 percent (FAO 2003).

- About **50 mg of ascorbic acid with the main meal** significantly enhances iron bioavailability (Cook and Monsen, 1977, cited in FAO 2003); little extra benefit beyond ~100 mg in one meal.
- **25 mg as lemonade at two meals a day doubled iron absorption** in one study (García et al., 1998, cited in FAO 2003).
- The effect is **greatest on meals with low iron bioavailability, such as vegetarian meals** (FAO 2003).
- **Timing matters:** the vitamin C source must be eaten at the *same meal* as the iron, not later.
- Indian sources of roughly a 50 mg portion include amla, guava, citrus and several green leafy vegetables. FAO notes **50–75 percent loss of ascorbic acid in cooking**, with pressure-cooking retaining the most.

**Household processing.** Germination, malting and fermentation are recommended specifically to overcome phytates (Chaudhary and Vir, 1994, cited in FAO 2003); fermentation degrades phytate and increases iron bioavailability in wholewheat bread (Brune et al., 1992). Soaking and sprouting pulses, and fermenting idli/dosa batter, are existing everyday Indian practices that work in this direction.

**Cookware.** Baking chapatti on an iron plate raised its iron content by 19 percent (Maingi and Narula-Sharma, 1972, cited in FAO 2003).

**For scale:** a meal containing roughly 85 g of meat produced the same increase in non-haem iron absorption as about 75 mg of ascorbic acid (Baynes and Bothwell, 1990, cited in FAO 2003) — one reason vitamin C pairing matters more in a vegetarian diet than a mixed one.

## Vitamin B12

B12 comes mostly from animal products and is **not reliably available from unfortified plant foods**. Lacto-vegetarians get some from milk and curd; the consideration is greatest for those eating little or no dairy. Confirming or correcting B12 status is a clinical matter, not a meal-planning one.

## Published Indian Reference Intakes

ICMR-NIN's 2020 expert group publishes both an EAR and an RDA. **The source itself states RDAs are not intended for menu planning for individuals and recommends the EAR for diet planning** — worth respecting rather than treating the higher RDA as a target.

| Nutrient (adults) | Male EAR | Male RDA | Female EAR | Female RDA |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Iron | 11 mg | 19 mg | 15 mg | 29 mg |
| Zinc | 14 mg | 17 mg | 11 mg | 13 mg |
| Calcium | 800 mg | 1000 mg | 800 mg | 1000 mg |
| Vitamin B12 | 2 µg | 2.2 µg | 2 µg | 2.2 µg |
| Vitamin C | 65 mg | 80 mg | 55 mg | 65 mg |
| Vitamin D | 400 IU | 600 IU | 400 IU | 600 IU |

*Source: ICMR-NIN, Nutrient Requirements for Indians (RDA and EAR), 2020.*

**On protein, note a deliberate distinction.** ICMR-NIN's general-population figures are an EAR of 0.66 g/kg/day and an RDA of 0.83 g/kg/day for healthy Indian adults, with a footnote that people eating cereal-based diets with lower-quality protein need about 1 g/kg/day. These are **general-population reference values, not athlete targets** — training athletes need more (see `football_nutrition.md`, following sports-nutrition guidance of roughly 1.4–1.8 g/kg/day). The figures are not in conflict; they answer different questions. ICMR-NIN also notes a cereal:legume:milk ratio of about 3:1:2.5 supports better protein quality.

## Practical Summary

- Pair the main iron-containing meal with a vitamin C source **at that same meal** — lemon over dal or rice, amla, guava, citrus, tomato, or vitamin C-rich greens.
- Move tea and coffee away from meals rather than drinking them alongside.
- Favour soaked, sprouted or fermented preparations where the household already uses them.
- Combine cereals with legumes, and include dairy where eaten, for protein quality.
- Treat persistent fatigue, unusual breathlessness or performance decline as a reason to see a doctor — not something to self-treat through diet or supplements.
